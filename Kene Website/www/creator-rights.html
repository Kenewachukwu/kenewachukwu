Client:
Mixtape

Product Design, Systems & Data Modeling

Project Background

Mixtape licenses creator-shot footage for brand campaigns, and every clip in the library carries real legal boundaries — how long a brand can use it, on which channels, and when it needs renewing. Before this project that information lived off to the side, checked by hand until something expired and nobody had noticed. This project put license status directly on the video itself: derived from the underlying rights data every time the page loads, never stored, never stale.

Never store a status — always derive it
The easy way to build a rights system is to add a status field to the video record and update it with a batch job. We didn’t do that. Every state on this page — the badge, the days-left counter, the button text — is computed at read time from the underlying license and rights views (v_source_video_rights, mt.creator_licenses), never written back onto the video. If a license lapses at 2am, every screen reflects it by 2:01, with nothing to keep in sync and nothing that can drift out of sync.
One block, six states, always in the same place
Rather than scatter licensing logic across different screens, we collapsed it into a single License block that lives in the same spot on every footage details page — directly under the creator, since a license is really a grant between a creator and a brand. It resolves to one of six states: Perpetual, Active (time-bound), Expiring Soon, Expired, Revoked, or No License on File. Whichever one applies, the block answers the same three questions at a glance: what’s the status, how long is left, and what can I do about it.
Expiring Soon gets a countdown, not a surprise
Seven days before a time-bound license lapses, the block switches to an amber countdown instead of waiting for the license to expire and explaining it after the fact. The goal was to make renewal a routine, low-stakes action — something a brand manager clears in passing — rather than a fire drill triggered by a legal complaint.
Expired means auto-paused, not deleted
When a license lapses, the asset is auto-paused rather than removed. The footage keeps its history and metadata — it simply can’t be pulled into a new cut or campaign until someone renews or replaces the license. Nothing about a creator’s past work disappears because a contract clock ran out.
Revoked is a kill switch, not a slow fade
Expired and revoked look similar from a distance — both block reuse — but they mean different things. A revocation is immediate and absolute: a creator or brand pulled rights outright, with no grace window. We styled it as a hard stop rather than the softer, time-based framing used for a natural expiry, so the two situations are never mistaken for each other.
An honest empty state
Some source footage was never actually licensed — uploaded for reference, or the paperwork fell through. Rather than force those into one of the other five states, “No License on File” gets its own plain, un-alarming treatment: it’s not an error, just a fact, with a clear path to attach a license if one exists.
Extending a license writes history — it never edits it
The Extend action doesn’t touch the existing license row. It inserts a new one that supersedes it — the prior license stays exactly as it was, immutable and fully auditable. On confirm, the pending auto-pause lifts automatically, because status is computed at read time; nothing about the video, its cuts, or the campaigns already built on it needs to be re-stamped.
Six license states, one page each. “Active — Time-Bound” is the sixth — visually identical to Expiring Soon minus the countdown badge — and isn’t pictured here.
